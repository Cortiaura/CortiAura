<p className="lead">Serotonin is often called the "happy chemical". Yet most of it is made somewhere you might not expect: your gut.</p>

**That fact has launched a thousand headlines about gut health and happiness.** As a gastroenterologist, I think the real story is more interesting than the myths. Here is what serotonin actually does, how your gut and its microbes are involved, and where the evidence stops.

## What is serotonin?

Serotonin is a chemical messenger. In the brain, it works as a neurotransmitter, passing signals between nerve cells. It is linked to mood, sleep, appetite and how we respond to stress [1, 2].

But the brain is not where most of it is made. About **90 to 95% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut**, mainly by specialised cells in the gut lining called enterochromaffin cells [3, 4].

## What serotonin does in your gut

In the gut, serotonin has nothing to do with feeling happy. It is a working chemical for digestion [4]:

- **Moving food along.** When food stretches the gut wall, serotonin is released and helps trigger the muscle contractions that push food through.
- **Fluid and secretion.** It helps control how much fluid the gut lining releases, which affects how soft or firm stools are.
- **Sending signals.** It activates nerves in the gut wall, including fibres of the vagus nerve that carry messages up to the brain [4].
- **Beyond the gut.** Serotonin released into the blood is taken up by platelets, the cells that help blood clot [3].

> Most of your serotonin never goes near your brain. It spends its life helping you digest your food.

## Your gut microbes help make it

Here is where gut bacteria come in. In a landmark 2015 study, mice raised without gut microbes had much less serotonin in their gut and blood. Certain spore-forming gut bacteria, from both mice and humans, prompted the gut lining to make more, which in turn affected gut movement and platelets [5].

So your gut microbes do not just live alongside you. They help set how much serotonin your gut produces. Our article on [gut microbes and the brain](/blog/gut-microbes-and-the-brain) explains the wider picture.

## Does gut serotonin make you happy?

Not directly. Serotonin **cannot cross the blood–brain barrier**, the protective lining that controls what enters the brain [3, 5]. The brain makes its own serotonin, separately, from an amino acid called tryptophan.

That does not mean the gut is irrelevant to the brain. There are at least three indirect routes:

1. **The vagus nerve.** Gut serotonin activates vagus nerve fibres that signal to the brain [4]. Read more in [what the vagus nerve does](/blog/what-does-the-vagus-nerve-do).
2. **Tryptophan.** Gut microbes affect how tryptophan from food is used: whether it becomes serotonin or is turned into other substances [6].
3. **Microbial chemicals.** Gut bacteria make many substances that act on the body, some of which may influence the nervous system [6].

### What studies in people show

In a 2019 study of more than 1,000 people in Belgium, those with higher levels of two types of gut bacteria (*Faecalibacterium* and *Coprococcus*) consistently reported a better quality of life. The finding held up in independent groups of people [7].

It is an intriguing link. But it shows an association, not cause and effect. People who feel well may eat, sleep and move differently, and all of those change gut bacteria too.

## Is serotonin really the "happy chemical"?

The label is too simple. Serotonin is one of several brain chemicals linked to mood and emotion, alongside others such as noradrenaline and dopamine [2]. Mood depends on many brain circuits, your sleep, your relationships, your health and your circumstances. No single chemical is "happiness".

## Medicines and lifestyle: not either-or

Some prescribed medicines work on serotonin in the brain. The NHS explains that antidepressants are thought to work by affecting brain chemicals such as serotonin [2]. Whether a medicine is right for someone is a decision for them and their doctor.

What is clear is that this is not a contest between "drugs" and "nature". Lifestyle habits support wellbeing for everyone, whether or not they take medicine. They are not a replacement for treatment that has been prescribed.

**If you take any prescribed medicine, do not stop it or change the dose without speaking to your doctor first** [2].

## What supports your gut, and your wellbeing

None of these is a "serotonin booster". Each has good evidence for general health and wellbeing.

1. **Feed your gut microbes.** The NHS recommends adults aim for 30g of fibre a day; most eat about 20g [8]. A wide variety of plant foods, beans, wholegrains, nuts and seeds supports a varied gut microbiome.
2. **Don't rely on "serotonin foods".** Turkey, eggs and cheese contain tryptophan, but eating more of it does not reliably raise serotonin in the brain, because tryptophan competes with other amino acids to get in [6].
3. **Move every day.** Being physically active supports both body and mood. UK guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week [9, 10].
4. **Look after your sleep.** Your body uses serotonin to make melatonin, the sleep hormone. Our guide to [sleep and gut health](/blog/sleep-and-gut-health) explains how sleep and digestion affect each other.
5. **Connect with other people.** Close relationships are the first of the NHS's five steps to mental wellbeing, alongside being active, learning new skills, helping others and paying attention to the present moment [10].
6. **Calm your nervous system.** Slow breathing shifts your body towards its "rest and digest" state within minutes. See [slow breathing: what the research says](/blog/slow-breathing-what-the-research-says).

## When to get help

If you have felt low, anxious or not yourself for more than two weeks, speak to your GP. If you have digestive symptoms that keep coming back or last more than three weeks, see your GP too. If you need help urgently, call NHS 111 or, in an emergency, 999.

## The bottom line

Most of your body's serotonin is made in your gut, where it helps you digest food, and your gut microbes help control how much is made. Gut serotonin does not reach your brain, but your gut and brain stay in close contact through the vagus nerve and other routes. Serotonin is not simply the "happy chemical", and lifestyle habits are not a substitute for prescribed treatment. Fibre, movement, sleep and connection remain the best-evidenced foundations for a healthy gut and a healthy mind.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is serotonin made in the gut?**
Yes. About 90 to 95% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, mainly by specialised cells in the gut lining called enterochromaffin cells [3, 4]. The brain makes its own, separate supply.

**Does gut serotonin reach the brain?**
No. Serotonin cannot cross the blood–brain barrier, so serotonin made in the gut stays in the body [3]. It can still send signals to the brain indirectly, for example by activating the vagus nerve [4].

**Is serotonin the happy chemical?**
Serotonin is one of several brain chemicals linked to mood, but calling it the happy chemical is too simple [2]. Mood involves many chemicals and brain circuits, and most of the body's serotonin is busy in the gut controlling digestion.

**Can food increase serotonin?**
Your body makes serotonin from tryptophan, which comes from protein foods. But eating more tryptophan does not reliably raise serotonin in the brain [6]. A varied, fibre-rich diet that supports your gut microbes is the best-evidenced approach for gut health [8].
