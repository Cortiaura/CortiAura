Have you ever felt butterflies before a big moment, or lost your appetite when worried? That is your gut and your brain talking to each other. Scientists call this two-way link the gut–brain axis, and research over the past two decades has shown how closely the two are connected.

## A two-way conversation

The gut–brain axis is a complex communication system that runs in both directions. Signals from the brain shape how the gut moves and works. Signals from the gut travel back to the brain, where they can influence emotions and even decision-making [1].

## How the gut and the brain talk

Messages travel along four main channels [1, 2]:

1. **Nerves.** The nervous system connects the brain directly to the gut wall.
2. **Gut hormones.** Specialised cells lining the gut release chemical messengers that the brain can sense.
3. **The immune system.** Immune cells in the gut wall pass on information about what is happening there.
4. **Gut microbes.** The bacteria living in your gut produce substances that nerves can detect and relay to the brain.

## The vagus nerve: the main line

The most important nerve link is the [vagus nerve](/blog/what-does-the-vagus-nerve-do). It is the main nerve of the parasympathetic nervous system, which supports the body’s “rest and digest” state. Around 80% of its fibres carry information from the body up to the brain, so it acts largely as a listening line from the gut [2].

## Why stress can upset your stomach

When you are stressed, the nerves that control the gut respond. This can change how the gut moves and what it secretes, which is why emotions so often show up as stomach symptoms [1]. NHS guidance notes that stress and anxiety can trigger or worsen gut symptoms such as diarrhoea, constipation and nausea in some people [3].

## Looking after your gut–brain connection

You cannot remove stress from life, but NHS guidance suggests ways to manage how much you face and how you respond to it [3]:

- Identify the stresses you can control and tackle those first.
- Try relaxation techniques such as slow breathing exercises.
- Stay active with walking, swimming, yoga or other exercise you enjoy.
- Make time for people and activities that you find rewarding.

If gut symptoms are ongoing, changing or affecting your daily life, speak to your GP.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the gut–brain axis?**
It is the two-way communication system between the gut and the brain. Signals from the brain shape how the gut works, and signals from the gut travel back to the brain [1].

**How do the gut and brain communicate?**
Through four main channels: nerves (especially the vagus nerve), gut hormones, the immune system and substances made by gut microbes [1, 2].

**Why does stress upset my stomach?**
When you are stressed, the nerves that control the gut respond, which can change how the gut moves and what it secretes [1]. NHS guidance notes that stress and anxiety can trigger or worsen gut symptoms in some people [3].

**How can I look after my gut–brain connection?**
NHS guidance suggests tackling the stresses you can control, trying relaxation such as slow breathing, staying active and making time for rewarding activities [3]. If gut symptoms are ongoing, speak to your GP.
