<p className="lead">Your heart races before a big meeting. An hour after a relaxed dinner, you feel calm and sleepy. Same body, two completely different modes.</p>

**Your nervous system is constantly switching gears, mostly without you noticing.** As a gastroenterologist, I see the effects of this switch every day, because your gut is one of the first places it shows. Here is how the two modes work, where the vagus nerve fits in, and how to help your body change gear.

## Your body's accelerator and brake

The part of your nervous system that runs things automatically, such as your heartbeat, breathing and digestion, is called the **autonomic nervous system**. It has two main branches that work like a car's accelerator and brake [1, 2]:

- **The sympathetic nervous system** is the accelerator. It drives the "fight or flight" response.
- **The parasympathetic nervous system** is the brake. It drives "rest and digest".

Both are always active to some degree. What changes is the balance between them, moment to moment [2].

## Fight or flight: built for action

When your brain senses a threat, the sympathetic system and stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol prepare you to act [1, 3]:

- your **heart beats faster and harder**
- your **breathing quickens** and airways widen
- **blood is sent to your muscles**
- your **pupils widen**
- stored **sugar is released** for energy
- **digestion slows down**, because it is not a priority in an emergency

This response is brilliant for a real physical threat. It is less helpful when the "threat" is an inbox, a traffic jam or a difficult conversation, because your body reacts in much the same way.

> Fight or flight was designed for a few minutes of danger. Modern life can keep it switched on for hours.

## Rest and digest: built for recovery

When the threat passes, the parasympathetic system takes over [1, 2]:

- your **heart slows**
- your **breathing settles**
- **saliva and digestive juices flow**
- your **gut moves food along** and absorbs nutrients
- your body turns to **repair and recovery**

The main nerve behind rest and digest is the **vagus nerve**. It carries most of the parasympathetic signals to the heart, lungs and gut [4]. Read more in [what the vagus nerve does](/blog/what-does-the-vagus-nerve-do) and [vagal tone explained](/blog/vagal-tone-explained).

## What this means for your gut

Your gut is highly sensitive to which mode you are in. Brain centres linked to the vagus nerve help control gut movement and secretion, while sympathetic signals tend to hold digestion back [5].

That is why stress can bring on a dry mouth, a knotted stomach or a sudden need for the toilet, and why you digest best when you are relaxed. Our article on [how stress affects your digestion](/blog/how-stress-affects-your-digestion) explains this in more detail.

## Fight, flight... or freeze?

There is a third response you may have felt: **freezing**. Faced with a sudden threat, people and animals often go still for a moment. The heart can briefly slow while the body stays on high alert, ready to act [6]. It is a normal part of the threat response, not a sign that something is wrong.

## When the accelerator gets stuck

The stress response is meant to switch on, do its job and switch off. Problems arise when it stays on.

Researchers describe the cost of long-running stress as **"allostatic load"**: the wear and tear that builds up when stress hormones stay raised for long periods [3, 7]. Over time, this can affect sleep, digestion, blood pressure and the immune system [3, 8].

The aim is not to get rid of stress. Short bursts of it help us focus and perform. The aim is to make sure your body can **shift back into rest and digest** afterwards.

## How to shift gears

None of these is a cure-all, but each has evidence behind it and is safe for most people.

1. **Breathe slowly, with a longer out-breath.** Breathing at about six breaths a minute increases vagus nerve activity within minutes [9, 10]. Try breathing in for four seconds and out for six. See [slow breathing: what the research says](/blog/slow-breathing-what-the-research-says).
2. **Move every day.** Regular activity helps your body handle stress better. UK guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week [11].
3. **Protect your sleep.** Sleep is when rest and digest does much of its work. Our guide to [sleep and gut health](/blog/sleep-and-gut-health) explains the link.
4. **Eat without rushing.** Sitting down for meals, away from screens, gives your body the cue to switch into digestion.
5. **Connect with people.** Time with friends and family is one of the NHS's five steps to mental wellbeing [12].
6. **Notice your signals.** A racing heart, shallow breathing or a tight stomach can be early signs that your accelerator is on. Noticing them is the first step to easing off.

## When to see your GP

Feeling stressed from time to time is normal. See your GP if stress is affecting your daily life, or if you have physical symptoms such as palpitations, chest pain, breathlessness or digestive problems that keep coming back. These need checking whatever you think the cause is. If you need help urgently, call NHS 111 or, in an emergency, 999 [13].

## The bottom line

Your nervous system has an accelerator (fight or flight) and a brake (rest and digest). Both are essential. The vagus nerve is the main driver of rest and digest, and your gut works best when it is in charge. You cannot avoid stress, but slow breathing, movement, sleep, unhurried meals and connection all help your body shift back into recovery mode.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between fight or flight and rest and digest?**
Fight or flight is run by the sympathetic nervous system and prepares you for action: your heart speeds up and digestion slows [1]. Rest and digest is run by the parasympathetic nervous system, mainly through the vagus nerve, and helps you recover: your heart slows and digestion speeds up [2, 4].

**Which nerve controls rest and digest?**
The vagus nerve. It carries most of the parasympathetic signals to the heart, lungs and gut, slowing the heart and supporting digestion [4, 5].

**Can you be in fight or flight all the time?**
The stress response is designed to switch on briefly and then settle. When stress keeps it switched on for long periods, stress hormones stay raised, which can affect sleep, digestion and long-term health [3, 7].

**How do I switch into rest and digest?**
Slow breathing with a longer out-breath is one of the quickest ways [9, 10]. Regular physical activity, good sleep, unhurried meals and time with other people also help the body settle over time [11, 12].
