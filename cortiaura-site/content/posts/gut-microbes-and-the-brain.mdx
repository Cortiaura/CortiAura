<p className="lead">Your gut is home to around 38 trillion bacteria. Scientists now think some of them are sending signals to your brain. The real question is how much of that has been shown in people.</p>

**The headlines run ahead of the evidence.** As a gastroenterologist, I am asked about gut bacteria and the brain more than almost anything else. Here is what the research shows, where it is still early, and what you can do today.

## Meet your gut microbes

Your gut contains bacteria, viruses, fungi and other microbes, together known as the gut microbiota. A widely cited 2016 study estimated that the body holds about **38 trillion bacteria**, roughly the same number as human cells, and that most of them live in the large bowel [1].

These microbes are not passengers. They break down fibre your body cannot digest, help train the immune system and make a wide range of chemicals that can act on the body [2].

## How gut microbes could talk to your brain

Your gut and brain are in constant two-way contact, a network called the [gut–brain axis](/blog/what-is-the-gut-brain-axis). Researchers have found several routes by which gut microbes may join that conversation [2]:

- **The vagus nerve.** This long nerve links the brainstem and the gut, and most of its fibres carry signals up to the brain. Sensors in the gut lining can detect microbial products and pass messages along it [3]. Read more in [what the vagus nerve does](/blog/what-does-the-vagus-nerve-do).
- **Short-chain fatty acids.** When gut bacteria ferment fibre, they make substances such as butyrate, propionate and acetate. These act on the gut lining, immune cells and nerves, and may affect the brain indirectly [4].
- **Hormones and gut chemicals.** About 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, and gut microbes help control how much is produced [5].
- **The immune system.** Microbes shape immune signals that can travel around the body. Our article on [the inflammatory reflex](/blog/the-inflammatory-reflex) explains how the vagus nerve helps keep inflammation in check.

> Your gut microbes do not have a direct line to your brain. They work through the same routes your gut already uses: nerves, hormones and the immune system.

One common myth is worth clearing up. **Serotonin made in the gut does not cross into the brain** [5]. It mainly acts locally, for example on gut movement. Your brain makes its own supply.

## What animal studies show

Much of what we know comes from mice, and some of it is striking.

- **Stress responses.** Mice raised without any gut microbes ("germ-free" mice) produced an exaggerated stress hormone response. Giving them normal gut bacteria early in life reversed it [6].
- **Behaviour and brain chemistry.** In a well-known 2011 study, feeding mice a particular *Lactobacillus* strain changed their behaviour and the activity of a calming brain chemical system (GABA receptors). When the vagus nerve was cut, the effects disappeared [7].

These studies show that, in animals, gut microbes can influence the brain, and that the vagus nerve is one of the routes [3, 7].

## What we know in people

Human research is much earlier, for three main reasons:

1. **Most studies show links, not causes.** Finding different microbes in different people does not tell us which came first. Diet, sleep, medicines and stress all change the microbiota as well [2].
2. **Mice are not people.** Germ-free mice have no equivalent in humans, and results do not always carry over.
3. **Trials are small.** Many human studies include a few dozen people and measure short-term changes.

So, a fair summary: the gut–brain connection is real, and gut microbes are very likely part of it. Exactly how much they influence how the human brain works is still being worked out.

## What you can do: feed your microbes

You do not need a test or a supplement to look after your gut microbes. The best evidence points to everyday food.

1. **Eat more fibre.** Fibre is the main food for gut bacteria. The NHS recommends adults aim for **30g a day**, but most adults eat only about 20g [8]. Beans, lentils, oats, wholegrains, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds are all good sources.
2. **Go for variety.** In the American Gut Project, which studied more than 11,000 people, those who ate more than 30 different types of plant a week had more varied gut microbes than those who ate 10 or fewer [9]. Herbs, spices, nuts and seeds all count.
3. **Try fermented foods.** In a 10-week randomised study of 36 healthy adults, a diet high in fermented foods such as yoghurt, kefir, kimchi and sauerkraut increased the variety of gut microbes and lowered markers of inflammation [10].
4. **Increase fibre gradually.** A sudden big change can cause wind and bloating. Build up over a few weeks and drink plenty of fluids.
5. **Look after the rest of the axis.** Sleep, movement and stress all affect your gut. Our guides to [sleep and gut health](/blog/sleep-and-gut-health) and [how stress affects your digestion](/blog/how-stress-affects-your-digestion) explain why.

What about probiotic supplements? Different products contain different strains, and most of the brain-related evidence comes from animal studies. If you are thinking about taking one, speak to a pharmacist or your GP.

## When to see your GP

Changing your diet is safe for most people, but see your GP if you have digestive symptoms that keep coming back or last more than three weeks, or if you notice blood in your poo, unexplained weight loss or difficulty swallowing. These need checking whatever you think the cause is.

## The bottom line

Your gut microbes are an active part of the conversation between your gut and your brain. In animals, they clearly influence stress responses and brain chemistry, partly through the vagus nerve. In people, the science is promising but early. The most reliable step you can take is also the simplest: eat more fibre, from a wide variety of plants.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many bacteria live in the human gut?**
About 38 trillion, according to a widely cited 2016 estimate [1]. That is roughly the same as the number of human cells in the body, and most of those bacteria live in the large bowel.

**Can gut bacteria affect the brain?**
In animal studies, yes: gut microbes influence stress hormones, behaviour and brain chemistry, partly through the vagus nerve [6, 7]. In people, the research is promising but early, and most studies show links rather than cause and effect [2].

**Is most serotonin made in the gut?**
Yes. About 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut, and gut microbes help control how much is produced [5]. But serotonin made in the gut does not cross into the brain, so it is not the same as the brain's own serotonin.

**What is the best way to look after your gut microbes?**
Eat more fibre and a wider variety of plant foods [8, 9]. The NHS recommends adults aim for 30g of fibre a day; most adults eat about 20g. Fermented foods such as yoghurt and kefir may also help increase the variety of gut microbes [10].
