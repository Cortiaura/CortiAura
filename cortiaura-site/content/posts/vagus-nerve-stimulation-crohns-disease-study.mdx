For decades, inflammation in the gut has been treated mainly with medicines that act on the immune system. But a growing body of research suggests the nervous system has a say too. One of the most interesting studies in this area was published in 2023 in the *Journal of Crohn’s and Colitis*, the journal of the European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation [1]. It asked a simple question: can stimulating the vagus nerve reduce inflammation in people with Crohn’s disease?

In this article, I explain the study step by step: the science behind it, what the researchers did, what they found, and, just as importantly, what it can’t yet tell us.

## First, what is Crohn’s disease?

Crohn’s disease is a long-term condition in which parts of the digestive tract become inflamed. It can affect anywhere from the mouth to the anus. Common symptoms include diarrhoea, stomach pain, tiredness and weight loss, and they tend to come and go in flare-ups [2]. There is currently no cure, but treatment can keep many people well for long periods. Treatments include anti-inflammatory medicines, medicines that calm the immune system, biological therapies and sometimes surgery [2].

Biological therapies have transformed care, but they don’t work for everyone. Many people don’t respond well enough, or find that a medicine stops working over time. The study authors note that fewer than half of patients reach lasting control of both symptoms and gut inflammation with current treatments [1]. That is why researchers are looking for entirely new approaches.

## The science: the body’s “inflammatory reflex”

You may know the vagus nerve as the main nerve of the body’s “rest and digest” system. It connects the brain to the heart, lungs and gut (you can read more in our article [What does the vagus nerve do?](/blog/what-does-the-vagus-nerve-do)).

In 2002, the neuroscientist Kevin Tracey described what he called the **inflammatory reflex**, in the journal *Nature* [3]. Put simply:

1. Sensory fibres in the vagus nerve detect inflammation in the body and send signals to the brain.
2. The brain sends signals back down the vagus nerve.
3. These signals reach immune cells and tell them to release fewer inflammatory chemicals, such as **TNF** (tumour necrosis factor) [3].

TNF is an important driver of inflammation in Crohn’s disease, and several widely used biological medicines work by blocking it. So if the nervous system can naturally turn down TNF, could stimulating the vagus nerve help? That is the idea this study set out to test.

## What did the researchers do?

The study was led by Professor Geert D’Haens at Amsterdam University Medical Centres, with teams in the Netherlands, Sweden, Croatia and Italy [1].

**Who took part:**

- 17 adults with moderate to severe Crohn’s disease had the device fitted, and 16 were included in the main results [1].
- All had already tried at least one biological medicine (anti-TNF therapy or vedolizumab) that hadn’t worked well enough or that they couldn’t tolerate. On average, they had tried more than two [1].
- Their disease was clearly active, confirmed by symptoms, by camera tests of the bowel (endoscopy), and by a stool test called **faecal calprotectin**, which measures inflammation in the gut [1].

**The device:**

- This was an **implanted** device, similar to one used for many years in epilepsy care. A small stimulator was placed under the skin of the chest, with a wire wrapped around the vagus nerve in the left side of the neck. This required an operation under general anaesthetic [1].
- After a two-week recovery period, participants switched the stimulation on themselves, starting with one minute a day. This was gradually increased to five minutes a day, and to five minutes four times a day for those who hadn’t reached remission by week 8 [1].

**How long and how it was measured:**

The main study ran for 16 weeks [1]. The researchers measured:

- **Symptoms**, using the Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (CDAI). This is a standard score that combines things like stool frequency, pain and general wellbeing. A drop of 100 points counts as a meaningful response, and a score under 150 counts as remission [1].
- **Gut inflammation**, using faecal calprotectin, endoscopy and tissue samples (biopsies).
- **Inflammatory chemicals in the blood**, called cytokines.
- **Quality of life**, using questionnaires.

## What did they find?

**Symptoms improved.** On average, the CDAI score fell by 86 points over 16 weeks, a change that was statistically significant [1]. Among the 12 people who received stimulation without any other new treatment, the average fall was larger, at 114 points [1].

- About half of all participants (53%) had a meaningful response, rising to 64% in the stimulation-only group [1].
- About a quarter (27%) reached remission, and just over a third (36%) in the stimulation-only group [1].

**Gut inflammation fell.** Average faecal calprotectin dropped from around 5,050 to around 1,970 micrograms per gram, a significant reduction [1]. Calprotectin is one of the most useful markers gastroenterologists have for tracking inflammation, so this was an encouraging sign that the change wasn’t only in how people felt.

**Inflammatory chemicals in the blood changed.** TNF fell by around 46% and another inflammatory messenger, interferon-gamma, by around 52% [1]. This fits with the inflammatory reflex idea. One other messenger, IL-17, rose, which the researchers will need to understand better [1].

**The bowel lining showed some improvement, but not consistently.** On endoscopy, 11 of the 15 people who had both a before and after test showed some improvement. However, the average change was not statistically significant, and only one person had complete healing of the bowel lining [1]. Biopsies from the small bowel (ileum) improved significantly, but this was based on just six people, and samples from the colon and rectum didn’t show a clear change [1].

**Quality of life improved for some.** Average quality-of-life scores improved, but not by enough to be statistically significant across the whole group. People who responded to treatment did report significantly better quality of life [1].

## Was it safe?

The authors concluded that vagus nerve stimulation was “generally safe and well tolerated” [1]. There was one serious problem linked to the study: an infection after the operation, which meant the device had to be removed before stimulation began [1]. This is a reminder that implanted devices carry the risks of surgery.

## The important limitations

As a doctor, I think it’s just as important to be clear about what this study can’t tell us:

- **It was small.** Only 16 people were included in the results.
- **There was no comparison group.** Everyone received stimulation, and everyone knew they were receiving it. Without a placebo or “sham” group, we can’t be sure how much improvement was caused by the stimulation itself. Symptoms in Crohn’s disease naturally vary, and expectation can influence how people feel.
- **Other medicines were allowed.** Some participants continued biological medicines, and many used steroids at some point during the study, which can make it harder to separate out the effect of stimulation [1].
- **It was short.** The main results covered only 16 weeks.
- **It was an early-stage study.** The researchers describe their findings as preliminary and needing confirmation in properly controlled trials [1].

In other words, this was a promising first step, not proof that the approach works.

## What has happened since?

The same scientific idea has since been tested in a much larger and more rigorous trial in a different inflammatory condition, rheumatoid arthritis. Published in *Nature Medicine*, the RESET-RA trial randomly assigned 242 people to active or sham stimulation with a small implanted device [4]. After three months, 35% of people receiving active stimulation met the main measure of improvement, compared with 24% receiving sham stimulation, a significant difference, although smaller than the researchers had hoped [4].

That trial is important because it was **sham-controlled**: it compared real stimulation with a dummy version. This is the kind of evidence that is now needed in Crohn’s disease.

## What does this mean for you?

- **The gut and the nervous system are deeply connected.** This study adds to the evidence that the vagus nerve is involved not only in digestion but also in how the body controls inflammation.
- **This is still research.** Vagus nerve stimulation is not an approved treatment for Crohn’s disease, and NICE guidance on managing Crohn’s disease is based on established medicines and surgery [5].
- **The device in this study was implanted.** The results don’t automatically apply to other forms of stimulation, such as devices worn on the skin.
- **Never stop or change your treatment without advice.** If you have Crohn’s disease or ongoing gut symptoms, speak to your GP or IBD team about any new approach before trying it.

## Key terms explained

- **Vagus nerve:** the main nerve connecting the brain to the heart, lungs and gut.
- **Inflammatory reflex:** a pathway through which the brain, via the vagus nerve, can reduce inflammation.
- **TNF (tumour necrosis factor):** a chemical messenger that drives inflammation, and a target of several Crohn’s medicines.
- **Faecal calprotectin:** a stool test that measures inflammation in the gut.
- **CDAI:** a score used in research to measure how active Crohn’s disease is.
- **Open-label:** a study in which everyone knows which treatment they’re receiving.
- **Sham-controlled:** a study that compares the real treatment with a dummy version, to separate true effects from expectation.
